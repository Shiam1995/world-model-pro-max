Method

One algorithm, used twice

Training is evolution strategies, not reinforcement learning and not gradient descent. The simulator already steps hundreds of karts at once, so the population is the batch: give every kart its own weights and a single vectorised rollout scores an entire generation. The top 12.5 % by fitness are averaged into the next generation's mean, and the search radius shrinks 3 % per generation.

Fitness is ground covered, charged for leaving the road — progress − 0.35 × offroad_frames × top_speed — so a policy cannot win by cutting a corner across the grass. The network is an MLP of 10 → 32 → 1 with tanh, 385 numbers, steering only; the throttle is held open.

Two deliberate anti-overfitting measures. The start point is randomised to any checkpoint on the lap each generation, so nothing wins by memorising one launch. And the physics itself is jittered every generation — turn rate ×0.70–1.45, acceleration ×0.80–1.25, top speed ×0.9–1.1, road width ×0.75–1.15 — because the sim is a fit, not a port, and its residual error is exactly what broke the first transfer attempt.

Campaign 01 · Luigi Raceway · Mario Kart 64

Five seeds, five drivers

Five independent evolution runs from different seeds, each about two minutes of CPU. They are separately evolved rather than copies, so their disagreement is real — that is the ensemble stage 2's confidence signal was meant to be calibrated against. Every one was then validated on the real ROM through the emulator, from the committed start-line savestate.

The world it trained in

A simulator with a stated error bar

Physics constants that exist in the decompilation — top speed per engine class, a ten-point acceleration curve, friction, gravity — are lifted directly. Everything else is fitted to scripted traces collected from the emulator itself: accelerate from rest, then hold each of three steering angles for 360 frames. The residual is reported rather than assumed.

A Luigi Raceway run is 384 karts × 900 frames × 80 generations ≈ 27.6 million kart-frames, delivered in under two minutes. The emulator manages roughly 100 frames a second. That ratio is the entire reason a fitted sim is worth building.

Campaign 02 · King's Cross figure-8 · Unreal Engine

The same loop, against a measured car

The second campaign trained a policy native to an Unreal track. The recipe is the one the handoff describes: record a single open-loop calibration run in Unreal (524 logged frames — a fixed script of straights and held steering angles), fit the kart sim to that trace, train in the sim, and drive the result back. Nothing about the vehicle is guessed; every number below is measured off the trace.

0.346 laps is not a failure rate. At the fitted top speed a 1400-step rollout can cover about 415 m, and the lap is 1003.6 m — so the training window ends before a lap can finish by construction. The three seeds covered 84 % of the theoretical maximum distance with zero off-road frames, and landed within 0.3 % of each other. What has not happened is the part that counts: none of them has been driven back in Unreal, so this campaign has a sim result and no real result.

One measured risk. The turn table only covers speeds down to 26.8 % of top — below that the sim extrapolates. An invented low-speed steering ramp is precisely the thing the README names as "the single thing that broke sim-to-real", because it lets a policy carry a large constant steering bias for free. The fix is more calibration coverage at low speed, not a tuned constant.

Negative results

What was not trained

  • No emulator-in-the-loop training Deliberate. The console is the oracle, never the training signal — which is what makes "trained for 112 s, then completed a real lap" a transfer claim rather than a fitting claim.
  • No gradient method, no RL Also deliberate: long episodes, delayed reward, 385 parameters, and a vectorised sim where the population is free. ES needs no credit assignment.
  • Confidence calibration The ensemble exists and one of its members demonstrably fails on hardware — the data to calibrate disagreement against outcomes is sitting in runs/fleet_eval.json. The calibration itself was never run. Still the biggest missing headline feature.
  • Generalisation across 21 courses The study crashed on launch and never produced a row: scripts/generalise.py hands a 1-D theta to act(), which indexes it as 2-D — IndexError: too many indices. A one-line reshape away from running. Until it does, whether a policy drives an unseen track is unmeasured.
  • Rollback search on hardware The model-free predecessor reached ~20 % of a lap, then the core stopped reporting PAUSED at frame 12178 and the run died. Superseded by the trained policy, kept because it needs no model at all.
