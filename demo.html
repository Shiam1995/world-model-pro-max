Mario to Sonic

A driving policy trained for 112 seconds in simulation, which never saw the emulator, completes a lap of Luigi Raceway on the real Nintendo 64 in 26.72 seconds. The physics constants come from the game’s own decompilation, fitted to the ROM, and the sim runs five thousand times faster than the console.

What was breaking it

Before that, a sim-trained policy drove into the barrier every time. Three suspects were ruled out by measurement — steering sign at the policy output, the yaw trig convention, and parameter error. The cause was none of them.

Measured on the real ROM

Stick deflection against yaw rate. MK64 has a wide deadzone and saturates by 0.8 — it is nothing like linear.

The turn was mirrored.

Replaying one fixed action script in both worlds: +0.8 steer moved the real kart toward +x and the sim kart toward −x. They agreed to 31 units while driving straight and were 678 apart after two turns. This was the one that mattered — and one open-loop replay found in a single run what hours of closed-loop policy evaluation had hidden, because a policy that fails can always be blamed on the policy.

Steering is non-linear, with a deadzone.

So the policy learned gentle ±0.27 corrections. On the real kart, 0.15 produces exactly zero yaw rate and 0.30 gives 5% of full lock. Its corrections did nothing, and it drifted wide into the barrier.

RDRAM is byte-swapped within each 32-bit word.

32-bit reads work little-endian, but every 16-bit field needs offset ^ 2. Heading and path index were reading neighbouring halfwords the whole time.

An agent found a bug before I did.

Where the start straight runs beside the return leg, nearest-point projection flips between them and pays out phantom progress. A policy learned to park there and farm it — 16.5 laps in 900 frames, when 0.8 is the physical maximum.
